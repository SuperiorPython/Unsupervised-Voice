# Unsupervised Voice — Dating Apps

**Draft:** v4 — revised Part 2 for clearer setup and visual gag  
**Working title:** *dating apps, aka love with a monthly subscription*  
**Target runtime:** ~6–7 minutes with brisk, natural delivery and short animation beats (verify by recording)  
**Format:** Spoken narration; bracketed scene directions and pauses are not read aloud.  
**Editorial guardrails:** This is commentary on product incentives and human choices. It does **not** allege dating apps actively prevent matches, that companies want users miserable, or that users are entirely to blame. Fictional sketches are clearly exaggerated jokes; don't present them as real company meetings.

## 1. The world's greatest pitch meeting

[Cheap conference room. Astronaut enters carrying a marker. One empty chair represents the investors. He is extremely pleased with himself. No channel intro.]

Okay, everybody. Hear me out. I have invented the perfect app.

[Writes each step on a whiteboard like he's unveiling a scientific breakthrough.]

Step one: find people who want a relationship.

Step two: introduce them to other people who want a relationship.

Step three: they meet somebody they actually like.

Step four: they fall in love.

Step five: they delete the app and never come back.

[He draws an enormous check mark and takes a proud step back. Looks at step five. Looks at the empty investor chair. Looks back at step five. Long double take. Very slowly erases it.]

...We'll workshop that last part.

[Cut. Astronaut looks directly into camera.]

Because I just realized I may have invented a business where doing your job REALLY well means your customers leave.

## 2. The idea is actually brilliant

[Conversational, genuinely positive.]

But jokes aside, dating apps are actually a pretty great idea.

Think about it. Before dating apps, your options were pretty much limited to whoever you happened to meet in your everyday life.

School, work, mutual friends, maybe somebody you worked up the courage to approach in public.

And look, that works for some people.

But what if you're shy? What if you're busy? What if you've already met everyone in your social circle?

[Beat.]

What, are you supposed to just start knocking on doors?

[Astronaut walks up to a random house, holding flowers. Door slams immediately.]

Yeah. Let's not do that.

Dating apps give you a way to meet people you probably would've never crossed paths with otherwise. People with similar interests, similar goals, or just someone you genuinely enjoy talking to.

And that's a good thing.

[Brief pause.]

The concept isn't the problem.

[Transition to a corporate meeting room.]

The problem is that somebody eventually has to figure out how to make money off it.

And that's where things start getting... interesting.

## 3. Congratulations, your love life has a pricing menu

Because you can't just build an app, release it into the wild, and pay everyone in good vibes.

Developers cost money. Servers cost money. Moderation costs money. Running a service costs money.

Apparently, romantic tension is not an accepted form of payment at the electric company.

So businesses have to figure out how to keep the lights on. Fair enough.

And that's where subscriptions, boosts, and paid visibility features come in.

[The astronaut opens a fictional dating app. A simple heart button unfolds into an absurdly long menu.]

“Congratulations! You've downloaded love.”

“Would you like to upgrade to LOVE PLUS?”

“Love Plus gives you extra visibility.”

“Love Premium lets you see more information.”

“Love ULTRA includes a complimentary emotional-support PDF.”

[Beat.]

Okay, I made that last one up. Please do not charge people for that.

But the weird feeling is real. You open an app to meet a person, and suddenly you're comparing subscription tiers like you're trying to buy cloud storage.

And, look, paying for features can change what tools you have available. It cannot make a stranger attracted to you.

You can buy a bigger megaphone. You cannot purchase chemistry.

## 4. The part that makes the room go quiet

And this brings me back to our poor little business plan.

What does success actually look like for a dating app?

If somebody wants a long-term relationship, finds one, and happily deletes the app, that's a pretty good result for that person.

But from the perspective of recurring subscriptions, a departing customer is also a departing customer.

[Boardroom scene. Astronaut wearing an obviously fake tie.]

“Ladies and gentlemen, wonderful news! We helped another couple find love!”

[Invisible executives cheer. Confetti.]

“They've deleted their accounts!”

[Confetti stops midair. Someone slowly takes down a party banner.]

Now before this becomes a conspiracy documentary, I'm not saying companies have a secret warehouse full of your perfect matches that they refuse to release until you pay fifteen dollars.

[Door labeled “SOULMATES — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.” He opens it. Inside: one mop and a folding chair.]

That would be ridiculous. Also, that's a terrible way to store soulmates.

A successful couple could recommend the app to friends. Happy users are good publicity. A company also has reasons to make its product genuinely useful.

So I'm not arguing that the company ALWAYS benefits when people fail.

I'm saying the business has to measure success somehow, and the easiest things to count—subscriptions, activity, time spent using the app—aren't necessarily the same as the outcome a person hopes for.

[Screen shows: USERS ONLINE: 12,000. Next to it: TRUE LOVE: ¯\\_(ツ)_/¯]

That's the uncomfortable little gap I keep coming back to.

## 5. Unfortunately, we are also in the app

And here's where I have to be fair, because it's not like companies invented every difficulty with modern dating.

They made the app. We brought the personalities.

People have different expectations. Different intentions. Different levels of effort. Some people aren't sure what they want. Some people know exactly what they want—and the requirements document is forty-seven pages long.

[The astronaut unrolls a comically long checklist. It rolls off-screen and down the hallway.]

Sometimes you get a match and then spend three business days trying to send one message.

[Phone displays “hey.” Astronaut stares. Day turns to night. A tiny plant grows through the desk.]

The app didn't type “hey” and then overthink the punctuation until the phone became an archaeological artifact.

That one's on us.

And I'm not even gonna go too deep into people's expectations, because then I'd be trying to psychoanalyze everybody in society, and I'm no therapist.

[An obviously fake framed certificate: “ABSOLUTELY NOT QUALIFIED.”]

All I'm saying is, software can introduce two people. It can't do the conversation for them. It can't create mutual attraction. It can't guarantee honesty or effort.

A match is an introduction, not an ending.

## 6. Back to the board

So I don't think dating apps are cartoon villains sitting around plotting how to keep the entire planet single.

I also don't think every bad date is the user's fault, or that paying for a premium feature is somehow immoral.

I think dating apps are a genuinely useful concept placed inside a business model that needs revenue, while being operated by people who are—how do I put this politely—people.

And sometimes those interests fit together beautifully. Sometimes they pull in different directions.

[Return to pitch room. Astronaut stands before the whiteboard. The erased fifth step is still faintly visible.]

Because if the goal is to help somebody find a person they don't want to swipe past, there's something a little strange about celebrating how long they keep swiping.

[He picks up marker, rewrites step five: “THEY DELETE THE APP.” Underlines it twice.]

Actually... no. I'm keeping that step.

[He faces the empty investor chair.]

I just have a feeling I'm never getting invited to another investor meeting.

[He starts packing up. A small sign lights up: “YOUR FREE TRIAL HAS ENDED.” He looks at camera. Cut to black.]

## Animation and delivery notes

- **Opening:** Let the board do the work. The double take, look to the empty chair, and erasing of step five are the main hook; avoid a music-heavy intro.
- **Comedy rhythm:** Deliver most of the narration conversationally. Save heightened delivery for a few phrases. The silence after the business revelation matters more than shouting.
- **Scenes:** Reuse the same room and mascot poses for multiple gags, rather than commissioning a new setting for every sentence. Distinguish real commentary from overtly fictional LOVE ULTRA and boardroom bits.
- **Factual care:** Product features vary by brand, tier, location, and time. Confirm any real price or brand-specific feature before showing it. Fictional subscription names are jokes.
- **No unsupported allegations:** The central claim is potential incentive tension, not that platforms deliberately withhold matches or manipulate specific users. Acknowledge word of mouth and customer success as valid business benefits.
- **Human nuance:** Don't imply all app users seek committed relationships; don't speculate about strangers' mental health. The therapist bit is an explicit boundary.
- **Pacing:** Record a rough take and time it. If it runs past seven minutes, trim explanations before trimming the pitch-meeting visual jokes.
